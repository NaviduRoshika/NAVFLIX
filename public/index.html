Films are mixed for a silent cinema, so dialogue sits low and explosions sit high. On a TV it is worse still: nearly all the dialogue lives in the centre channel, and folding six channels into two turns that channel down while the effects add together. This closes the gap: quiet parts up, loud parts down. Takes effect the next time something starts playing.

Works out resolution, codecs, audio and subtitle tracks and real running time for files that have not been looked at yet, a few at a time. Your own disk only, and nothing leaves the machine. It stops the moment you press play.

Collects the posters and episode stills still missing, so you do not have to press Get artwork once per folder. This one uses the network, a handful of requests at a time. Turn it off if you would rather NAVFLIX only went online when you ask it to.

Posters are fetched when you press Get artwork and, if you have left the switch above on, in the background too. Either way they are cached on disk, so it happens once. Without a key it uses the free, keyless Cinemeta catalogue, which is IMDb-backed and covers mainstream films well. A free key from themoviedb.org improves coverage for obscure ones.

Phone remote

Off by default. While it is off NAVFLIX only listens to this computer. Turning it on makes the server reachable from your home network, so pair only devices you trust. The remote can play, pause, seek, skip and switch collection; it cannot reach Settings, your folders, or the file browser. Takes effect next start.

The whole collection

Mark everything watched is for a show you had already seen before NAVFLIX met it. Start it over puts every episode back to unseen for a rewatch, and keeps what has been read and fetched: resolutions, cast, artwork and any subtitle offsets you tuned.

Backup

One file holding every collection, position, tag and subtitle offset, the only part of NAVFLIX that cannot be rebuilt. Keep it anywhere; it carries no pairing code, no tokens and no API key, so it is a file rather than a credential. Artwork and subtitles are left out too, since they can always be fetched again. Restoring replaces your progress, and writes what it replaced into data/backups/ first.

Storage

Deleting a video leaves its saved position behind, and removing a folder leaves its cached posters and subtitles on disk. Nothing clears either up. This looks for both and shows you exactly what it found before removing anything. A folder it cannot read is left completely alone, since an unplugged drive looks the same as a deleted one.

Danger zone

Erasing also discards everything the background worked out about these files (resolutions, tracks, cast and crew, subtitle offsets), all of which has to be read and fetched again. To watch it again, use Start it over.
